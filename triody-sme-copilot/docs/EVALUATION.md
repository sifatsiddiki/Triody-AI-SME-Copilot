### What changed since the previous version

The previous version scored 76.7% strict and 80.0% relaxed, a tie with the cheapest-eligible baseline. Four of its six misses were refusals caused by the affordability check. Three of those four were stock purchases (E1, E2, E3). The fourth (A3) was a cash gap.

The fix: stock is sold, and the sale repays most of the loan. For stock finance of up to 12 months, the check now counts the cost of credit plus 25% of the amount (in case some stock does not sell), not the whole amount. Longer stock loans are judged like any other loan. The 25% is a planning assumption, and the result is sensitive to it: any value from 0% to 25% gives 86.7% here, 30% gives 83.3% and 50% gives 80.0%, because some stock cases sit close to the limit. On the held-out set, 0% to 25% gives 85.7% and 30% gives 78.6%. 25% is the highest value that keeps the result, so it is the most cautious choice, but it was picked after seeing these numbers. This change was made after seeing the misses, so these 30 cases no longer test it fairly.

A3 is still a miss, and should be. The business would repay about £1,180 a month from £900 of free cash flow. The label says recommend. A careful adviser could refuse, so the label may be wrong, but it was not changed.

### Cases where the matcher gave an answer outside the acceptable list

- A3: expected supplier_credit (acceptable ['supplier_credit', 'overdraft']), matcher chose None. Reason for the label: Small, short gap and a supplier relationship: a supplier extension is cheapest.
- A5: expected wc_loan (acceptable ['wc_loan']), matcher chose overdraft. Reason for the label: Mid-size gap, 10 days to arrange: the working capital loan is cheaper than the cash advance.
- D2: expected term_loan (acceptable ['term_loan', 'young_loan']), matcher chose credit_line. Reason for the label: Lowest rate, 30 days is enough.

Shop B showed ranges that were too narrow, so the forecast now sets its range width from the shop's own past errors. Shop C, a florist, was built before that change, as a clean test of it. Shop C then showed about 65% coverage, and its misses were used to design two more fixes (below), so shop C is no longer a clean test. Shop D, a Shenzhen tea shop priced in yuan, was written before those fixes were run on it. Its first run exposed a bug (also below), which was then fixed, so shop D is not fully clean either. Three changes since that version:

1. One-off sales (a wedding order, a bike sale) no longer lift the expected level or reshape the week. Each day is capped at 1.5 times the typical day before the level, trend and weekday pattern are fitted. Found on shop C: after a wedding order, the forecast stayed too high for days.
2. Day-to-day noise is sampled from up to 12 weeks instead of 4, as a ratio to the typical day around it. A 4-week window often held no one-off at all, and the ranges then left them out. Found on shop C.
3. A monthly bill seen 3 times is now projected on its date. It needed 4 before. With 6 months of history, a monthly rent or payroll seen only 2 or 3 times was spread thinly over every day. Found on shop D, where rent and payroll are large and monthly. This is a plain bug fix, and it also improved shops A to C.

Earlier history, shop A: weekly sales error 11.3% against 11.09% for repeat-last-week, range coverage 35.4% at day 14 and 74.4% at day 28. On shops B and C it crashed, because it divided by zero on days the shop is closed. The figures quoted earlier (10.85% against 10.36%, 58% at day 14) used only 12 origins. See `evidence/previous_version_results.json`.

Changes: closed days are found from the data; the weekday pattern and level use the last 4 weeks; the trend fades more slowly; each simulated future draws its own sales level and its own bill amounts; irregular payments such as quarterly rent are spread at their long-run rate; and the range width comes from the shop's own past 14-day errors.

### What this shows, and what it does not

- On all four shops the balance forecast beats the naive one at day 14 and day 28, by 29% to 69%. The model knows when each bill falls, and the naive forecast does not.
- On weekly sales, the model beats both baselines on shops A, B and C. On shop D it does not: 7.1% against 6.6% for the flat 28-day mean. Shop D's sales are smooth, and its holiday peak in early October pulls the 4-week trend. Simple baselines are hard to beat on smooth sales.
- Ranges now err on the wide side on shops A, B and D (84% to 98% against 80%). A cash alert that is too cautious costs the owner less than one that is too relaxed, but it can warn too early. Shop C is still a little narrow at day 14 (about 71%).
- Remaining cause of misses on shop C: its monthly seasonal steps (for example 14% down from July to August) arrive as a jump the model cannot see coming, and errors bunch in time. The fix is a yearly season, which needs at least 12 months of history.
- Every model over-forecasts on average when a shop heads into a seasonal low. Six months of history cannot show a yearly season.
- All four shops are synthetic and written by the team. Results on a real business will differ.
